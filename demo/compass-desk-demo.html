Analytical question

How can I make this solo concerto for clarinet work, with a clarinet soloist and a pre-recorded orchestra tracked in Logic and Pro Tools, built from audio-processed parts played on various clarinets and saxophones?

1 · Form
Late Beethoven-like concerto, 3 movements
2 · Intention
Make pre-recorded audio match the soloist's intensity and virtuosity, so a soloist can play ensemble music with dynamic audio production
3 · Forces
Solo clarinet in B♭ and A, with a screw-in pickup to be amplified
5 · References
Reich, New York Counterpoint · Navarro, Clarinet Concerto No. 3 · Ravel, Piano Concerto · Berlioz, Symphonie fantastique
6 · Open
Which processing chains turn a family of clarinets into quasi-orchestral instruments
7 · For
Anthony Kalanick, UW-Madison DMA clarinet, 15 to 20 minutes, recital in spring 2027
8 · Status
Nothing is written
GuideOne-sheet▼
Piece ID

A three-movement concerto in which processed clarinet and saxophone audio is not accompaniment but the structural argument the soloist plays against.

Unique characteristics

The processing chain is a second performer, not support.

The screw-in pickup changes the soloist's timbral identity mid-piece.

Unexplored territory

The processing chain is undefined, and that is a question of form, not of sound design.

No material yet, and the references pull in four incompatible formal models.

Recommended path

Test the processing chain against one 30-second formal moment before building the whole track.

Constitution12 settled · 7 open▼
Settled decisions
1

The pre-recorded track is a full orchestral voice built from processed clarinet and saxophone recordings, so one soloist can perform the piece.

2

Two phases: write a real concerto for clarinet and orchestra first, then replace the orchestra with processed clarinet recordings.

4

Fast, slow, fast, with Beethoven's formal logic as the primary structural influence.

5

Lendvai's framework governs harmony, form and motive at once, a distinct system for each movement.

6

Movement 1 opens with the soloist alone; the track accumulates until the orchestra takes over and the soloist rests for the first time.

10

Tonal axes: B♭ to D, then A to C♯, then B♭ to A to B♭. The semitone between B♭ and A connects the whole piece.

11

Switching between the B♭ and A clarinets is a structural harmonic event, not just a practical one.

Open territory
  • The pickup's states (acoustic, clean, processed) as formal parameters
  • The cadenza: placement, length, relation to the track
  • The form of movements 2 and 3

Theme and Variations

The double-grand-staff idea is structurally smart but only works if the upper staff carries the actual theme melody, not just the theme's register. The synthesis argument depends on the listener hearing the theme as a fixed object while the variations move beneath it. The risk is a texture painting rather than a formal argument.

The theme (mm. 1-16) is not a closed periodic theme in the Beethoven Op. 26 sense. It is more rhapsodic, with meter changes (4/2, 3/2, 4/2 again) and an accelerando and ritardando built in. It is already through-composed at the theme level, which gives the variations permission to be abstract.

Variation 1 (mm. 17-41) takes the theme's lyrical half-note upper voice and its slow harmonic rhythm. The bell quality lives here.

Variation 2 (mm. 42-72) takes the eighth-note figure from mm. 5-8, the moment where the theme accelerates, and makes it the structural spine.

what do you think about creating double grand staff piano parts
TP
The Pianist

The Rachmaninoff precedent is worth being precise about. He uses four staves not to imply four hands but to separate voices that would be unreadable on two. The notation is a reading aid, not a density claim. Your constitution already gives each staff its own identity, which is exactly when four staves justify themselves.
