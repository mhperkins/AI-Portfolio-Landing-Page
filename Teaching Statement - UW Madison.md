# Teaching Statement — Maxwell Perkins

## Philosophy

Fifteen years of private and group music instruction taught me that teaching is mostly diagnosis. A student who cannot execute a passage is rarely missing information; they are missing the specific piece of structure that would let the information land. My job is to find that piece and build toward it, not to repeat the explanation louder. Group lessons sharpened this further: teaching four students of different levels the same session means building one shared structure flexible enough to differentiate inside it, in real time. That is the same problem a mixed-level university classroom presents, just at a different scale.

I have not yet taught in a university classroom. What I bring is fifteen years of one-on-one and group diagnostic teaching, the model applied music training actually runs on, and a body of independent work, The Composer Compass, that is itself a teaching system: an evaluation rubric I designed to make explicit what "good" AI-assisted composition means, so that judgment usually left implicit becomes something a student can learn, apply, and argue with. Rubric design for creative work is a teaching skill before it is anything else. I have already built one, tested it across multiple runs, and refined it based on where it failed. That is teaching infrastructure, not a substitute for it.

## Goals for Digital Music Technology Curriculum

**Year one:** design and pilot a course treating generative AI tools in music not as instruments to master but as systems to evaluate. Students would build or use an AI composition or production tool, then design their own rubric for judging its output against a stated aesthetic or technical goal, defending that rubric to peers. This is the structure I built for myself in The Composer Compass, adapted for a classroom.

**Ongoing:** develop a project-based sequence where students pair a creative deliverable (a piece, a production, an arrangement) with a technical one (a tool, a script, an evaluation framework) so that the two disciplines reinforce each other from week one rather than living in separate courses.

**Mentorship:** for graduate students working at the intersection of composition and AI, my value is direct experience building the infrastructure, not just using the output of someone else's model. I can mentor students through the actual engineering decisions: how to structure an evaluation protocol, how to ground a generative system in reference material instead of letting it run on an ungrounded prior, and how to read the failure modes of a model honestly instead of overselling its output.

## Translating Studio Teaching Into the Classroom

My concrete plan for year one is to co-teach or shadow at least one course with a current faculty member before leading my own section, specifically to pick up the institutional and classroom-management norms that a lecture format needs and one-on-one instruction does not. This is a fast translation, not a rebuild: the underlying skill, reading where a student's understanding breaks down and rebuilding from there, transfers directly. What needs adjusting is scale and format, not the core competency.

## Inclusive Teaching

Group instruction across skill levels is, in practice, differentiated instruction: the same lesson has to reach a beginner and an advanced student in the same room without either one disengaging. I would carry that habit into curriculum design for digital music technology specifically, a space where students arrive with wildly uneven technical backgrounds (some have written code, most have not) and where the fastest way to lose a room is to assume a shared starting point that is not there.
